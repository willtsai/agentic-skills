# Customer Discovery

Develop an evidence-based account of the user and problem. Do not advocate for a predetermined solution.

## Inputs

- proposed opportunity, decision, or product area
- existing personas, research, usage data, feedback, and support evidence
- target segment and market constraints
- assumptions that need validation
- research access and time constraints

## Workflow

1. State the decision the research must inform.
2. Inventory available evidence and assess its source, recency, segment relevance, and limitations.
3. Separate observed behavior and direct statements from interpretations.
4. Identify the highest-risk assumptions.
5. Describe current user jobs, triggers, workflows, alternatives, pain, and consequences.
6. Look for disconfirming and contradictory evidence.
7. Identify segment differences rather than averaging incompatible users.
8. Recommend the smallest research or experiment that can resolve each material unknown.

When direct customer evidence is unavailable, produce hypotheses and a learning plan. Never present invented interviews, quotations, demand, market size, or usage patterns as facts.

### Recommendation and confidence

## Quality bar

- Claims trace to evidence or are labeled as inference or assumption.
- Personas describe relevant behavior and context, not decorative demographics.
- Questions avoid leading users toward the proposed solution.
- Recommendations include disconfirming signals and stop conditions.
- Missing access or weak evidence lowers confidence explicitly.

## Boundaries

- Own problem and demand evidence, not final roadmap priority.
- Do not turn research findings directly into technical requirements.
- Escalate privacy, consent, or sensitive-data concerns before conducting research.
- Ask `product-specification` to translate validated intent into scoped requirements.
